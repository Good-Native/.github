# Naming at Good Native

How we name the things we make, and why.

## Why naming matters to us

The company name is the strategy. **Native** means two things at once: software
that belongs where it lives — integrating into a store or a stack without bloat,
breakage, or demands for attention — and the natural world of the place we're
from. **Good** means well-made and honest. Every product name should do the same
double duty: borrowed from nature, describing software that behaves like a good
native.

A name is the first promise a product makes. Ours promise quiet competence:
the thing sits in your environment, does its job, and doesn't shout.

## The principles

### 1. Name the behaviour, not the features

We name things after something in the natural world whose _behaviour_ mirrors
what the software _does_. The metaphor is functional, never decorative. A
sundew passively and continuously captures whatever lands on it; a mopoke sits
still through the night and calls only when there's something to say. The test
is one sentence: "_X_ does _Y_ in nature; this software does _Y_ to _Z_." If
you can't write that sentence, it's the wrong name.

### 2. Native to place

Names are drawn from the Australian natural world — flora, fauna, and plain
English words rooted in nature and growth. This is identity, not theme: we're
founded in Castlemaine, Victoria, and the names say so without a flag or a map.
A species name and a common word like _bloom_ are equally valid expressions of
the same principle; what matters is the natural behaviour behind it, not which
register it comes from.

Every name anchors to a specific native species, whether or not the species is
in the word itself. Where the name is the species (_sundew_, _mopoke_,
_currawong_, _paperbark_), the anchor is built in. Where the name is a plain
word, the species carries the identity visually — _bloom_ is embodied by the
banksia in its logo, _hover_ by the blue-banded bee, both native to
Castlemaine. Prefer species found where we're founded; the closer to home, the
truer the name.

### 3. Quiet confidence

Understated over clever. No puns that need a beat to land, no startup suffixes
(-ly, -ify, Hub, AI), no compound mashups. A good name means little on first
hearing and rewards the person who asks — the rationale is a small gift for
the curious, not a joke that has to work in the pitch.

### 4. One word, everywhere

Short, easy to say, spell, and type. The name must survive every surface it
will live on: repo, CLI command, package name, subdomain
(`goodnative.co/x`), app store listing, a support call spoken aloud, and a
log line. Lowercase must look natural; if the name only works with a capital
letter and a logo, it's a wordmark, not a name.

### 5. The rationale ships with the name

Every named thing carries a short "The name" note in its README: the namesake,
its behaviour, and the one-line mapping to what the software does. Two or three
sentences, written when the name is chosen. A name whose reason is folklore is
a name that drifts. The note covers the chosen name only — candidates that
didn't make it stay in the naming discussion, not the README. The
[organisation profile](https://github.com/Good-Native/.github) carries each
name's story in its overview panel, so the family's rationale lives in public
view, not in a register.

### 6. Respect

Use common English species names. Words from Aboriginal and Torres Strait
Islander languages are not ours to claim as brands; where a common name has
Indigenous origins (as many Australian species names do), we use it as the
species' widely-used common name and nothing more — no logos trading on the
word's origin, no origin stories we don't own.

### 7. Family, not formula

Siblings, not a template. New names should feel at home in the existing list
without copying its pattern — same temperament (quiet, natural, behavioural),
different character. When a README mentions the family, it lists the whole
family, consistently.

## Scope — what gets a name

- **Products and tools get names.** Anything with an audience — merchants,
  developers, the public — earns a real name and a rationale.
- **Plumbing gets labels.** Docs sites, infra, and internal repos take plain
  functional names (`help`, `.github`). Don't spend a species on plumbing.
- **Features get descriptions.** Inside a product, features are named for what
  they do (_Notify Me_, _Releases_). One brand per product; a feature with its
  own brand is a product that hasn't been scoped yet.

## Choosing a name

1. **Start from behaviour.** Write down what the software actually does in one
   verb-led sentence. Shortlist natural namesakes whose behaviour matches —
   behaviour first, aesthetics second.
2. **Say it out loud.** In a support call, in a standup, in "have you tried
   \_\_\_?". Discard anything that needs spelling out.
3. **Check availability.** `goodnative.co/x`, the package registry it will
   live on (npm, PyPI), the Shopify App Store if relevant, obvious trademark
   collisions, and existing software with the same name in an adjacent space.
4. **Check the family.** Would it look at home in the org repo list? Same
   temperament, no near-duplicates in sound or meaning.
5. **Write the rationale.** If the "The name" paragraph writes itself, ship
   it. If it needs three drafts, go back to step 1.

Names are durable. Renaming breaks links, packages, muscle memory, and trust —
a name is only revisited for a legal conflict or a genuine scope change, never
for fashion.
